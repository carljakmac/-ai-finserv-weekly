The Week at a Glance

The week the AI-capex bill landed on the tape — and the plumbing got its governance:

  • The Linux Foundation launched the x402 Foundation — with Visa, Mastercard, Stripe and AWS aboard — to govern autonomous agent-to-agent stablecoin payments, days before a Visa–Artemis report mapped a hybrid future of cards for macro-commerce and stablecoins for micro-commerce.
  • Semiconductors had their worst stretch since spring after TSMC lifted its 2026 capex guidance to $60–64B, sharpening the market's "prove the returns" question on AI infrastructure.

  • The buy side's AI problem turned out to be contracts, not capability — 69% of large asset managers say data-licensing terms are the biggest barrier to feeding research into their models.
  • The EU AI Act reaches full applicability on Aug 2, but the Digital Omnibus quietly reshuffled the high-risk timeline; the BIS warned the AI build-out is starting to rhyme with past investment manias.
  • Deployment muscle — not model choice — became the enterprise battleground, as AWS, OpenAI and Anthropic all doubled down on forward-deployed engineering.

01

Key Takeaways
  1. Scope agent-to-agent payment standards now. With x402 governance live and Visa mapping a card-plus-stablecoin hybrid, agentic-commerce rails are formalising faster than most treasury roadmaps assume.
  2. Treat AI-linked equity concentration as a live risk, not background noise. The capex-versus-revenue debate hit the chips this week; audit the AI exposure your allocations don't advertise.
  3. Draw your human/agent line deliberately. Banks are now assigning agents real jobs across trading, treasury and wealth — decide which tasks are human, which are software, and which are hybrid, before attrition decides for you.
  4. Re-open your data and research licences for machine-readable use. The buy side's binding constraint on AI is contractual, and approvals still take four to six months.
  5. Confirm which AI systems are due Aug 2 versus 2028. The EU AI Act reaches full applicability, but the Digital Omnibus extended parts of the high-risk timeline — "delayed" is not "exempt."
  6. Weigh deployment muscle, not benchmark scores, when you buy. With frontier models converging, forward-deployed engineering is becoming the real differentiator for regulated buyers.

02

Deals, Funding & M&A

Fintech's H1 Scorecard: $28.6B Raised, Deal Count Down 25% — Capital Concentrates on AI

Crunchbase's first-half recap, out this week, shows global fintech startups raised $28.6B in H1 2026 — up almost 23% year-on-year — even as the number of deals fell 25.7% to roughly 1,605 rounds. The money is pooling: the US absorbed about $15B (52% of the global total), the UK $2.7B and India $1.9B, while investors wrote fewer but far larger cheques into wealth management, financial infrastructure and enterprise automation. Ramp's $750M round at a $44B valuation in June and Stripe's $159B February tender headline a market where a small set of scaled, AI-native players is pulling away from the field.

So What: "Money up, deals down" reads as a healthy market until you notice what it means for buyers: the vendor shortlist is consolidating to a handful of well-capitalised leaders per category. That's good for procurement clarity and bad for negotiating leverage. Lock in commercial terms and exit rights now, while your preferred vendor still has competition in its lane.

Aethon Launches AI-Driven Hedge Fund With $50M and an Ex-Citadel CTO

New York-based Aethon Fund launched this week with $50M in initial capital, pitching institutions on a systematic strategy that pairs proprietary market signals — built from years of retail-investor data — with AI-driven trade execution, while keeping humans over research and risk. Founder George Kailas says the signals capture patterns traditional quant models miss; CTO Dave Lauer is a former high-frequency trader at Citadel and Allston who has advised US regulators on market structure. In an unusual move, Aethon says it will publicly publish examples of how selected stock signals are generated while keeping its investment process proprietary.

So What: A $50M launch is small, but the design is the point: lean, AI-native, and staking differentiation on a non-consensus data source (retail flow) rather than the filings-and-transcripts feeds every fund now trains on. That's a direct answer to the crowding problem this newsletter has tracked since May. Allocators screening emerging managers should stop asking "do you use AI?" and start asking "what data do you have that peers' models can't see?"

03

New Products & Use Cases

Linux Foundation Launches x402 to Govern Agent-to-Agent Payments; Visa Maps the Hybrid Future

On July 14 the Linux Foundation formally launched the x402 Foundation, an open-governance body — with roughly 40 members including Visa, Mastercard, Stripe and AWS — to steward a protocol built on the long-dormant HTTP 402 "Payment Required" code, letting AI agents settle stablecoin payments autonomously (in practice, largely on Coinbase rails today). Two days later, a Visa–Artemis report, "Agentic Payments from the Ground Up," argued for a hybrid model: card rails keep handling consumer-scale "macro-commerce" while stablecoins take the sub-cent, high-frequency "micro-commerce" that runs between software. Both flag the same gap — chargeback and dispute rules built for human-speed commerce don't yet exist for agents transacting thousands of times an hour.

So What: Two weeks after the Open USD coalition, the same players are standing up neutral, foundation-level governance — a sign this is becoming infrastructure, not a pilot. But the honest headline for risk teams is the missing dispute layer: on-chain agent payments sit outside card chargeback protections, and liability for an unauthorised machine payment is legally unsettled. If you're scoping agentic commerce, spending limits, wallet segmentation and transaction logging are the only controls that currently exist.

Revolut Builds "PRAGMA" — a Proprietary Foundation Model for Banking

Forbes reported that Revolut is building its own foundation model, PRAGMA, designed to understand financial behaviour holistically rather than through a patchwork of point tools. By folding transactions, app usage, investments and support interactions into a single connected system running on NVIDIA GPUs across its ~70 million users, Revolut says the model has lifted fraud detection by roughly 65%, improved credit-risk prediction by 16%, and boosted product recommendations by 41% — with insights from one function compounding across the rest of the customer journey. It's a bet that a unified, bank-specific model beats stitching together generic AI tools.

So What: Revolut is trying to own a model trained on its own behavioural data — a strategy only scale-plus-clean-data players can attempt, and a close cousin of Travelers' proprietary insurance LLM we covered last week. For incumbents, the takeaway isn't "build your own foundation model" (almost none should); it's that fragmented tooling leaves compounding value on the table, and Revolut's cross-function lift is the benchmark your unified-data business case will face.

04

Use Cases in Action

Wall Street Turns AI Agents Into "Digital Coworkers" Across Trading, Treasury and Wealth

A Reuters report this week (July 13) found major US banks moving past chatbots to assign autonomous agents real jobs — vetting new clients, reconciling treasury, supporting trading, and nudging advisers when a client's annuity is about to mature. Morgan Stanley's head of AI for wealth management, Koren Maranca, said the bank will begin testing round-the-clock, client-facing digital assistants later this summer; Goldman Sachs is building agents with Anthropic for trading, transaction accounting and onboarding; and Citi is developing an AI-powered virtual wealth "team member." Separately, PYMNTS reported that 51% of banks are now piloting AI agents specifically to lift productivity.

So What: The framing has shifted from "AI helps our people work faster" to "which jobs belong to humans, which to software, and which to a hybrid." That's an org-design decision, not a tooling one. Wealth and operations leaders should sort their workflows into those three buckets on purpose — because if you don't draw the human/agent line deliberately, vendor roadmaps and attrition will draw it for you.

JPMorgan's AI Asset-Allocation Agents Beat a 60/40 Portfolio in Backtests

Bloomberg reported (July 9) that JPMorgan has been testing AI investing agents that rotate between stocks and bonds as market conditions shift — and that early backtest results beat a conventional 60/40 portfolio. The same coverage pointed to a Harvard-led study finding machine-learning models can now anticipate roughly 71% of active mutual-fund trades, with most of the remaining value hiding in the 29% they can't predict. It's the clearest sign yet that banks are testing whether AI can make one of the industry's most consequential calls — how to allocate capital — not just assist the humans who do.

So What: When a bank's AI beats the industry's default allocation in backtests, the "does it work?" debate on capital allocation is effectively over — the live question, as we flagged last week, is edge decay. Pair the backtest optimism with the crowding data in this issue's Equity Pulse: the more funds converge on similar models, the faster any single edge erodes. Treat an AI allocation model as a depreciating asset that needs continuous refresh, not a one-time build.

Buy-Side AI Runs Wide — but Broker-Research Licensing Is the Bottleneck

A Substantive Research–Aiera survey of 35 of the largest global asset managers, reported by Traders Magazine on July 16, found 77% now run organisation-wide deployments of general-purpose generative AI. Yet the value chain jams at the data layer: 77% said broker research is the most valuable input to feed machine-readable into their internal AI, followed by earnings transcripts (57%) and market data (42%) — but 69% cite broker and data licensing restrictions as the single biggest barrier to piping those feeds in, ahead of compliance and entitlements (54%). For many firms, approving and onboarding a model still takes four to six months.

So What: The buy side's AI problem is no longer capability — it's contracts. The research and data licences most funds signed years ago never contemplated feeding content into an LLM, and that clause is now the gating item for research automation. Heads of research and market data should re-open licensing terms explicitly for machine-readable, AI-ingestion use — and budget for a multi-month approval cycle rather than assuming a fast switch-on.

Insurance: Underwriting Becomes the Fastest-Growing AI Speciality as Capital Chases AI-Native Carriers

A Sollers study found underwriting is now the fastest-growing IT speciality in insurance, with four in ten insurers already using AI within their underwriting functions — a decisive move from experiment to production. The capital markets agree with the operators: recent InsurTech analysis shows AI-native carriers and vendors capturing essentially all of the sector's fresh investment, with startup carrier Corgi reporting more than $40M in annual recurring revenue within roughly a year of full regulatory approval. The through-line: AI is no longer the pitch in insurance — it's the plumbing, embedded in claims, pricing and fraud workflows.

So What: When "AI-powered" stops being a differentiator, execution becomes everything — and the clearest proof point is an AI-native carrier reaching eight-figure ARR in under a year. It's the flip side of Travelers' build-your-own-model bet from last week: whether you build or buy, the winners are the ones combining institutional data with frontier capability. Incumbents should benchmark cycle time and straight-through-processing against these clean-sheet competitors now.

AI Equity Pulse: Bubble Watch

Weekly market analysis — written from the perspective of a senior equity analyst

Week in Numbers

S&P 500 7,533.77 (Thu close), off ~1% on the week  |  Nasdaq Composite 25,881.95, down ~2%+  |  Dow 52,552.97  |  Semiconductors (SMH) third weekly decline in four, down ~8% on the month  |  Micron −8–9% intraday Wednesday  |  Apple new all-time high on the software rotation

The Capex Bill Comes for the Chips

The index-level moves were modest, but the composition told the story. Semiconductors — the heartbeat of the AI-capex thesis — sold off for a third week in four, with the VanEck Semiconductor ETF down roughly 8% on the month. The proximate trigger was a good-news-is-bad-news moment: Taiwan Semiconductor beat on Q2 earnings but raised its 2026 capital-expenditure guidance to $60–64B (from $52–56B), and the stock fell about 4% as investors read the higher spend as a demand signal they're no longer willing to pay up for on faith. Micron dropped 8–9% intraday mid-week; AMD, Intel and Marvell shed 5%-plus. Underneath, capital rotated — into mega-cap software (Apple hit a record high, Amazon, Alphabet and Microsoft rose ~3%) and defensives, with IBM a reminder that even blue-chips get punished for a soft outlook, falling 25% on weak software and infrastructure guidance.

The Smart Money Keeps Trimming — and the BIS Reaches for the History Books

The institutional signals hardened this week. Bloomberg reported that Chinese hedge funds sitting on huge AI-linked gains — Shanghai Everlead Capital, up 164% this year through May — are trimming optical-communications and advanced-packaging positions, saying they're on "high alert" for froth. Separately, systematic managers gave back roughly a quarter of their year-to-date returns in recent weeks as chipmaker volatility and heavy Korean-market leverage hit crowded trades, while Hazeltree's H1 crowding report (600-plus funds, ~16,000 securities) confirmed positioning has concentrated further into semis and AI infrastructure. Adding a central-bank voice, the Bank for International Settlements published a working paper this week, "The AI investment race," ranking the current build-out among the largest technology-driven investment booms in US history — and asking whether it ends the way canal, railway and dot-com manias did. A companion BIS bulletin flagged that business development companies have lent ~$115B to software firms, exposure that isn't yet priced for AI-driven revenue uncertainty.

Analyst View

This is not a call to sell AI exposure — it's a call to know exactly how much you own, and to notice that the debate has moved. For two years the market rewarded ownership of AI capacity; this week it started demanding proof of AI returns, and a raised capex number was treated as a cost rather than a promise. The gap the bears keep pointing to — hundreds of billions in annual infrastructure spend against a far smaller pool of monetisable enterprise AI revenue — is exactly what a rising TSMC capex line makes harder to ignore. The playbook that keeps recurring in the institutional flow: keep exposure to the infrastructure layer where cash flows are real, hedge tail risk cheaply, and rotate marginal capital into the broadening trade — software mega-caps, defensives, small-caps — while auditing the concentration you didn't choose. Q2 earnings season over the next few weeks is the test that either validates the capex or punishes it.

Regulation & Compliance

EU AI Act Hits Full Applicability Aug 2 — but the Digital Omnibus Reshuffles the High-Risk Clock

The EU AI Act becomes fully applicable on August 2, 2026, when the bulk of its obligations — governance rules, penalties, and general-purpose-AI duties already live since August 2025 — take full effect. But the picture has shifted: under the Commission's "Digital Omnibus" simplification package, the transition period for high-risk AI systems embedded into regulated products has been extended to August 2, 2028. On July 7, Brussels also published an EU Action Plan on Cybersecurity and AI, and the EBA has reconfirmed the Act is designed to complement — not duplicate — existing frameworks such as DORA, CRR and PSD2, with national supervisors folding AI-Act checks into normal SREP cycles.

So What: Don't read "extended to 2028" as breathing room. The core obligations — transparency, human oversight, logging and risk management for credit scoring, fraud and automated decisioning — still bite, and supervisors will assess them inside existing exam cycles from this year. The practical move is to build AI-Act compliance on top of your DORA setup rather than as a separate silo, and to confirm precisely which of your systems fall under the extended timeline versus the ones due now.

FCA Innovation Update: "Targeted Support" Scaling, New Climate Sandbox, Supercharged Cohort 2 in Flight

FinTech Global's regulatory round-up for the week ending July 10 captured the FCA's latest innovation stock-take. The new Targeted Support regime — the lighter-touch category between information and full advice that Scottish Widows and others have been trialling — went live in April; seven firms have now been approved and 23 more have applied or are engaged via the Pre-Application Support Service. The regulator also confirmed a new Climate Scenarios Sandbox cohort for later in 2026, alongside the NVIDIA-powered Supercharged Sandbox (whose agentic-AI Cohort 2 closed to record applications on June 1) and its Scale-up Unit.

So What: Targeted Support moving from proposal to seven live approvals is the concrete UK development to watch — it's the regulatory gap that lets AI-driven guidance reach mass-affluent customers without triggering full advice obligations. Life, pensions and platform firms weighing a hybrid guidance product should track the first movers closely; the design patterns they get approved will set the template, and the PASS queue suggests the window to be an early shaper is open but filling.

New Partnerships

The "Deployment Wars": Frontier Labs and Clouds Race to Own the Services Layer

A clear pattern hardened this week: the enterprise-AI battle has shifted from raw model capability to who can wire a capable model into decades of messy institutional workflow. AWS stood up a roughly $1B internal AI deployment organisation; OpenAI's Deployment Company acquired integration specialist Northslope (reported July 13); and both OpenAI and Anthropic are now running private-equity-backed deployment ventures aimed at embedding forward-deployed engineers inside client operations — the Palantir playbook, adopted industry-wide. For regulated FS buyers that can't easily rebuild their tech stacks, this services layer is becoming the real product.

So What: With frontier models now close enough on capability, the differentiator is deployment muscle — and that changes how banks and insurers should buy. The question is no longer "which model scores highest?" but "who will put engineers alongside my team to make it work in production, and on what terms?" Mid-market firms without in-house AI engineering should treat a lab's or integrator's forward-deployed offering as a core selection criterion, not an add-on.

SAP and SAP Fioneer Push "AI Can Only Act on Integrated Data" for Financial Institutions

SAP and SAP Fioneer sharpened a partnership message this week aimed squarely at financial institutions: the shift underway is from systems that store and report information to systems that act on it in real time, orchestrating end-to-end processes — and AI can only create value on top of integrated data, real-time access and standardised processes. The pair are combining SAP's cloud and data infrastructure with Fioneer's financial-services depth to help institutions modernise core systems, ahead of their SAP & SAP Fioneer Financial Services Forum 2026.

So What: It's a vendor message, but the diagnosis is right: most institutions still run on fragmented foundations, and that — not model choice — is what caps AI value. The uncomfortable implication is that the winners over the next five years won't be the firms experimenting with the most models, but the ones that fix their data plumbing first. If your AI pilots keep stalling, the problem is probably upstream of the model.

Research & Talent Moves

The Academic Front: NBER Papers Put Hard Numbers on the "AI Premium" and Agentic Alpha

A cluster of NBER working papers dated July 2026 gave the AI-in-markets debate some rigour. "AI Premium" (Borri, Liu & Tsyvinski) uses 380 trillion tokens of real AI consumption to build an AI factor and finds firms whose returns co-move most with frontier AI use earn a large, heterogeneous premium — a value-weighted long-short strategy returning about 64 basis points a week, though the effect is notably absent in emerging markets including China. A separate paper by Koijen & Levy shows optimised agentic AI systems can more than double the explained variation in earnings-announcement returns (lifting R² from roughly 8% toward 20%) while producing human-readable economic explanations, and Alex Chan's "Risk Design" reframes how AI-driven prevention reshapes selection in insurance markets.

So What: Two useful signals for investment teams. First, "AI exposure" is becoming a measurable, priced factor — worth understanding as a portfolio tilt you may already hold unintentionally (see this issue's Equity Pulse). Second, the agentic-asset-pricing work suggests the frontier isn't just accuracy but interpretability: models that explain why a price moved are what survive a risk committee. Read the interpretability angle as the direction of travel, not the raw backtest numbers.

Talent Watch: Monzo Co-Founder Tom Blomfield Reportedly Joins Anthropic

Industry reporting this week indicated that Tom Blomfield — co-founder and former CEO of UK neobank Monzo — has joined Anthropic, reportedly working with its AI compute team, alongside high-profile hires including Andrej Karpathy. The moves land as Anthropic is reported to be running at roughly $47B in annualised revenue and preparing an IPO said to be targeted for October. A fintech founder who helped build one of Britain's best-known challenger banks crossing into a frontier AI lab is a notable meeting of the neobank and AI-infrastructure talent streams.

So What: Talent flow is a leading indicator, and it's pointing from fintech operating roles toward the AI-infrastructure layer. That's a two-sided signal for FS leaders: the labs building the tools you depend on are actively hiring people who understand banking from the inside — and your own best product and engineering talent is now a target for them. Retention of AI-fluent operators just got harder, and more strategic.
