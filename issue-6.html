AI in Financial Services

Week of July 23 – 30, 2026  |  Issue #6

Created by Carl Jakobsson

The Week at a Glance

The week the AI-capex reckoning arrived — for markets and regulators at once:

  • The Nasdaq 100 fell into a technical correction, down 11% from its record, as a chip rout and questions about whether AI spending will ever pay off dragged the Dow to its worst day since April 2025.
  • Singapore's MAS named the sustainability of the AI investment boom a key risk to financial stability, noting AI-linked firms are now ~40% of the S&P 500's market cap — as Bloomberg tallied three red flags in a single week.
  • The EU AI Act's 2 August milestone arrives Sunday, but the Digital Omnibus has quietly deferred the headline high-risk deadline to December 2027 — changing what firms actually need ready on Monday.
  • Ant International raised a $1.2bn Series A for cross-border agentic commerce; AI-native insurer Corgi's valuation hit $4bn on its third raise in three months.
  • Q2 bank earnings made one thing clear: AI is showing up as spending, not savings — with Jamie Dimon projecting $1tn in annual AI capex and confirming AI-driven headcount cuts.
  • The deployment layer got a name — Anthropic, Goldman and Blackstone launched Ode — while MAS and the UK both moved to put governance around agentic finance.

01

Key Takeaways
  1. Audit your AI-linked concentration this week, not next quarter. The capex-sustainability question moved from FinTwit to central banks and a Nasdaq correction — the exposure you didn't choose is now the risk you have to explain.
  2. Re-check what the EU AI Act actually requires on 2 August. The Digital Omnibus deferred standalone high-risk obligations to December 2027; governance, GPAI and penalty provisions still apply now. Don't remediate against the wrong deadline.
  3. Build interim agentic-AI governance regardless of jurisdiction. MAS's runtime safeguards, the UK Adoption Plan and a 93%-give-agents-autonomy statistic all point the same way: controls can't wait for one tidy rulebook.
  4. Treat "agent-ready" payment rails as a live procurement question. Ant's $1.2bn raise is aimed squarely at cross-border agentic commerce — ask incumbents what their agent settlement story is.
  5. Reframe the AI business case around revenue and capacity, not headcount savings. The banks reporting this quarter pointedly refused to sell AI as a quick cost cut — follow their lead with your board.
  6. Weigh deployment muscle when you buy. With Ode now live and named, the frontier labs are competing on who embeds engineers in your workflow — that, not benchmark scores, is the real selection criterion.

02

Deals, Funding & M&A

Ant International Raises ~$1.2bn to Scale Cross-Border Agentic Commerce

Ant International closed a Series A of roughly $1.2bn — the week's largest fintech round — to strengthen its cross-border payments and agentic-commerce capabilities. The financing drew existing backers Ant Group and Alibaba alongside a group of international institutions, with capital earmarked for global expansion and new products across merchant payments, account management and SME-focused inclusive finance. The raise lands in a quarter where average fintech deal size jumped from about $27m to $35m as investors concentrated bets — and it plants a serious flag in the race to build the settlement layer for machine-to-machine transactions.

So What: A raise this size aimed explicitly at agentic commerce tells you where the smart payments money thinks volume is heading. For treasury and cross-border teams, it reinforces a question we keep coming back to: can your current provider settle agent-initiated, cross-border transactions at scale and speed? Ant is building for a world where the answer has to be yes — and pricing the option accordingly.

AI-Native Insurer Corgi Hits $4bn Valuation — Its Third Raise in Three Months

Corgi, the San Francisco AI-driven insurtech, secured fresh capital at a $4bn valuation — nearly double its late-May mark — in a further extension of its Series B, reportedly its third fundraise in under three months. Size and investors were undisclosed, but the cadence itself is the signal: this is the AI-native carrier we flagged reaching eight-figure ARR only weeks ago, now being repriced almost fortnightly even as broader AI sentiment wobbles. The round stands out precisely because it runs counter to the caution creeping into public AI markets this week.

So What: Private markets are still paying up for AI-native insurance execution even as public AI valuations crack — a divergence worth watching. For incumbent carriers, Corgi's velocity is a competitive clock: every extension funds more underwriting share taken from slower-moving books. Benchmark your quote-to-bind cycle and straight-through rates against clean-sheet rivals now, because the capital backing them isn't waiting.

Flex Raises $70M for an AI-Native Private Banking Platform for Business Owners

San Francisco fintech Flex raised $70M in a Series B1 led by Halo Fund, extending a $60M Series B from December to build out a private-banking platform aimed at high-net-worth business owners. The proposition folds banking, spend management and treasury into a single AI-driven experience for founders and owner-operators who fall between retail wealth tools and traditional private banks — a segment incumbents have historically served with people, not software.

So What: This is the mass-affluent-to-HNW squeeze playing out in real time: AI-native challengers targeting exactly the clients private banks find too small for a dedicated relationship manager but too valuable to ignore. Wealth and business-banking leaders should decide deliberately where AI-delivered service replaces human coverage in their own segmentation — before a well-funded challenger sets that boundary for them.

03

New Products & Use Cases

Agentic Assistants Move From Chat to Proactive: Maverick, BROK and MoTA

A cluster of launches this week showed the "AI assistant" evolving from something a professional opens to something that opens the conversation. Maywood publicly launched Maverick, an agentic platform for senior bankers that inverts the model — it prompts the user, running inside a firm's existing email, calendar, file storage and CRM to surface prioritised outreach and ready-to-review drafts, with a human approval gate on any external action and SOC 2 Type II certification. Neobanx demoed BROK, an explainable AI banking agent, at StartUpNV in Las Vegas, while Nasdaq-listed Waton Financial readied a Q3 public beta of MoTA, a multi-agent system that brings hedge-fund-style portfolio construction to individual investors.

So What: The design shift matters more than any single product. Proactive agents that watch signals and initiate work change the control question from "did the AI answer correctly?" to "what is it allowed to do before a human sees it?" The vendors getting traction — Maverick's approval gate, BROK's explainability — are the ones treating that as the headline feature, not an afterthought. Evaluate agentic tools on their guardrails first, their cleverness second.

04

Use Cases in Action

Q2 Bank Earnings: AI Shows Up as Spending, Not Savings

Across a blockbuster Q2 — JPMorgan's net income rose 41% to $21.2bn, Goldman posted its best quarter ever — AI took up more airtime on earnings calls than in any prior quarter, and executives were careful not to sell it as a quick cost cut. The convergent message, per PYMNTS's read of the calls, was that technology spend is now a permanent feature of operating budgets. Jamie Dimon projected AI capital expenditure could reach $1tn next year, while also confirming AI is already reducing headcount in some functions; Goldman credited an AI-assisted dealmaking surge for part of its investment-banking strength.

So What: The banks with the most credible AI stories deliberately anchored them to revenue and capacity rather than a payroll line — a useful template for anyone whose board wants a cost-savings headline. But Dimon saying "job cuts" and "$1tn" in the same season is the tension of the moment: AI is simultaneously a growth investment and a workforce restructuring. Get ahead of both narratives internally before they're framed for you.

62% of Financial Firms Have Deployed AI Agents — and 93% Let Them Act Autonomously

A Cloud Security Alliance study resurfaced in industry coverage this week put hard numbers on how far agentic deployment has already run: 62% of financial-services firms have put AI agents into production, and of those, 93% grant the agents some degree of autonomy to act. The figures reframe the FCA's Mills review warning about "agent-led" journeys as a present-tense reality rather than a forecast — most firms aren't debating whether to give agents autonomy, they've already done it, often ahead of the governance to match.

So What: The 93% figure is the uncomfortable one. Autonomy without a mature runtime control layer is exactly the gap MAS's SAFR framework and the EU AI Act's logging requirements are built to close. If your firm is in that 93%, the board-level question isn't "should we use agents?" — it's "can we show, for every autonomous agent in production, what it's permitted to do and how we'd stop it?" If you can't answer that today, that's the priority.

AI Equity Pulse: Bubble Watch

Weekly market analysis — written from the perspective of a senior equity analyst

Week in Numbers

Nasdaq 100 in technical correction, ~11% off its record  |  S&P 500 7,316.15 (Wed), −1.5% on the day  |  Dow 51,594.14, −2.2% (−1,153 pts) — worst day since April 2025  |  Chips Micron −9.9%, Applied Materials −8.4%, AMD −5.5%, Nvidia −3.5%; SK Hynix −14.7% and Samsung −13% in Asia  |  30-yr Treasury highest yield since 2007  |  Brent above $90

The Reckoning Arrives

For two months this column has argued the market would eventually stop rewarding AI capacity and start demanding AI returns. This was the week it happened. The Nasdaq 100 slid into a technical correction, roughly 11% below its record, led by a brutal semiconductor rout — Micron off nearly 10% on Wednesday, Applied Materials down more than 8%, and a violent Asian session that saw SK Hynix fall almost 15% and Samsung 13% on reports of a Chinese chip-making breakthrough and a wave of cheaper Chinese AI models. Layered on top: a Federal Reserve that held rates but drew three dissents favouring a hike, 30-year Treasury yields at their highest since 2007, and Brent back above $90 on renewed Middle East tensions. The trigger was less any single print than a dawning question — investors watching big US firms deepen a web of AI-linked investments and keep spending even at the expense of free cash flow, and asking what the return actually is.

When a Central Bank Says "Bubble Risk," Listen

The most important voice this week wasn't a hedge fund — it was a central bank. Singapore's MAS, releasing its annual report, named the sustainability of the AI investment boom a key risk to global growth and financial stability. Managing Director Chia Der Jiun laid out the concentration starkly: AI-connected firms now make up around 40% of the S&P 500's market capitalisation and more than 30% of the MSCI EM Asia index, while AI-driven electronics account for over 70% of Asia's export growth this year, up from 46% in 2024. His warning was symmetrical — a long, productive boom would spill over into demand and inflation, but if payoffs disappoint, hyperscalers moderate spending and markets reprice. Bloomberg, separately, tallied three red flags in a single week: the MAS warning, the Nasdaq's down-arrow, and a public petition urging the US to slow AI's advance. And Jamie Dimon's projection of $1tn in annual AI capex now reads less as a bullish flourish than as the exact number the bears are asking firms to justify.

Analyst View

A correction is not a crash, and this isn't a call to capitulate — the after-hours split on Wednesday told the real story, with Microsoft up sharply on its fastest cloud growth in four years while Meta fell on doubts it can monetise its AI spend. That is the market finally discriminating between AI earners and AI spenders, which is healthy, not catastrophic. The through-line for FS allocators: concentration is now a supervisory-level concern, so size your AI-linked exposure as if a regulator will ask you to defend it, because one just modelled the downside for you. Watch three things into next week — hyperscaler capex guidance from the remaining Big Tech reports, whether the Chinese cheaper-model narrative sticks or fades, and the long end of the curve, because an AI drawdown colliding with a re-acceleration in yields is a very different risk than either alone. The debate has permanently changed from "how much AI do you own?" to "which side of the earner/spender line is it on?"

Regulation & Compliance

EU AI Act's 2 August Milestone Arrives — but the Headline High-Risk Deadline Has Moved

Sunday, 2 August marks the point at which the EU AI Act's governance rules and penalty provisions take full effect, joining general-purpose-AI obligations that have applied since August 2025. But the deadline financial firms spent the spring bracing for has shifted: under the Digital Omnibus simplification package, obligations for standalone high-risk systems under Annex III — credit scoring, insurance risk assessment, fraud detection — have been deferred to 2 December 2027, with AI embedded in regulated products pushed to August 2028. The core substantive duties (transparency, human oversight, logging, risk management) are unchanged; only the clock has moved, and formal publication was expected ahead of the August date.

So What: The risk now is remediating against the wrong date in both directions — scrambling for a 2 August high-risk deadline that has moved, or quietly downing tools because "it's been delayed." Neither is right. Confirm precisely which of your systems are standalone Annex III (December 2027) versus embedded (August 2028) versus governed by the provisions that bite Sunday, and keep building the audit-trail and human-oversight infrastructure — the deferral is time to do it properly, not permission to stop.

Singapore's MAS Stands Up an Industry Taskforce Against AI-Driven Cyber Threats

On 28 July, MAS and the Association of Banks in Singapore established the AI-Driven Cyber and Technology Risk Taskforce (ACT), an industry-wide body to strengthen collective resilience against frontier-AI threats — the ability to identify and exploit vulnerabilities and automate attacks at scale. The Taskforce will uplift sector cyber-defence knowledge and run proof-of-concept trials of AI-enabled defensive tools. It arrives alongside MAS's earlier SAFR runtime-safeguards framework for AI agents and a new Future of Finance Institute, positioning Singapore as one of the most active jurisdictions building practical guardrails for agentic finance.

So What: MAS is pairing a financial-stability warning about AI with concrete resilience machinery — the "defender's advantage" argument made operational. For CISOs and tech-risk leaders, the ACT model of pooled, regulator-convened AI cyber defence is the template to raise with your own supervisor. The firms that engage early with these collective-defence initiatives will shape them; the rest will inherit whatever standard emerges.

UK's Financial Services AI Adoption Plan Lands With 10 Recommendations

The government's AI Champions in financial services presented their Financial Services AI Adoption Plan this month, setting out a vision for widespread, safe AI adoption across the sector and ten recommendations spanning government, the regulators and industry. It follows a Treasury Committee report on AI in UK financial services and builds on the earlier joint statement from the Bank of England, FCA and HM Treasury on frontier-AI cyber and operational resilience, plus CMORG's June firm-level frontier-AI guidance. Together they signal a UK approach that leans pro-adoption while tightening expectations on resilience and accountability.

So What: The UK is trying to have it both ways — actively encouraging adoption while layering on resilience expectations — and for firms that's an opportunity to shape a still-forming framework. Map your AI governance against the Adoption Plan's ten recommendations and the CMORG guidance now; being able to show alignment with the emerging UK consensus is fast becoming table stakes in supervisory conversations, not a differentiator.

New Partnerships

The Deployment Layer Gets a Name: Anthropic, Goldman and Blackstone Launch "Ode"

The enterprise-AI services firm first floated in the spring has launched under its official brand: Ode with Anthropic. A standalone company combining Anthropic's models with a team of AI engineers and operators, Ode is built on the foundation of Fractional AI (acquired in May) and led by CEO Chris Taylor and CTO Eddie Siegel. Its backer list reads like a who's-who of finance — Blackstone, Hellman & Friedman and Goldman Sachs as founding partners, with Goldman Sachs, General Atlantic, Leonard Green, Apollo, GIC and Sequoia in the consortium. It's the concrete embodiment of the "deployment wars" this newsletter flagged a fortnight ago; separately, systems integrator UST also announced a tie-up to bring Claude into enterprise operations.

So What: Ode institutionalises the model of embedding forward-deployed engineers inside client operations — with private-capital backers who also own the portfolio companies that become its first customers. For mid-market banks and insurers without deep in-house AI engineering, ventures like Ode (and OpenAI's equivalent) are becoming the realistic path to production. The procurement question is shifting from "which model?" to "whose engineers, on what terms, with what exit rights?"

Research & Talent Moves

WEF & NVIDIA: AI in Finance Is Moving to the Front Office — and Budgets Keep Rising

The World Economic Forum's "AI Playbook for Financial Services 2026," drawing on NVIDIA's State of AI in Financial Services research, documents a decisive shift of AI use cases toward front-office, customer-facing functions rather than back-office automation. The spend trajectory is unambiguous: 83% of surveyed organisations expect AI budgets to rise in 2026, with 44% planning increases of more than 10%. The report's framing is notably human-centred — AI as a strategic leadership issue where people must remain firmly in the lead — a useful counterpoint to a week dominated by capex-sustainability anxiety.

So What: The move to the front office is the strategically significant shift: back-office AI saves cost, but front-office AI touches revenue and customer experience — and carries far higher conduct and explainability stakes. If your AI portfolio is still weighted to internal automation, you're optimising the cheaper half of the opportunity. But front-office deployment is exactly where the Consumer Duty and suitability exposure lives, so pair the ambition with the governance.

The Chief AI Officer Goes Mainstream in Banking

The AI leadership role is formalising at the top of the house. Lloyds Banking Group named Sameer Gupta to the new position of chief data and AI officer to lead its AI strategy, while Commonwealth Bank of Australia appointed Ranil Boteju — formerly group chief data and analytics officer at Lloyds — as its first chief AI officer. The moves reflect a broader pattern of banks elevating AI from a divisional technology concern to a board-adjacent, enterprise-wide mandate with a named, accountable owner.

So What: Creating a chief AI officer is how an institution signals that AI has crossed from experiment to enterprise discipline — and gives regulators a single accountable executive to hold responsible, which is increasingly what supervisors want. The cross-pollination (Lloyds talent heading to CBA) also shows how thin the pool of proven AI-transformation leaders still is. If AI ownership in your firm is diffuse across the CTO, CDO and COO, expect that to become a supervisory question.
