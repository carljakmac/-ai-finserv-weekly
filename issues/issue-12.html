AI in Financial Services

Week of October 10 – 16, 2026  |  Issue #12

Created by Carl Jakobsson

The Week at a Glance

The week the watchdog said the quiet part out loud — and the banks proved both halves of it:

  • The IMF named an AI market correction a "salient risk" to global financial stability, flagging stretched valuations, concentration, and the "circular financing" in which AI firms bankroll one another. AI stocks slid to a near one-month low on debt fears before a bond-led rebound.
  • Q3 bank earnings showed the dual bet in the open: lenders are financing the AI build-out and adopting it internally at once. Citi reported products shipped up to 40% faster and tech-vendor costs down 30%; Goldman's Solomon admitted "no one knows" how compute demand evolves.
  • The Anthropic IPO firmed to a November listing — marketing reportedly from the week of 9 November, trading before Thanksgiving — as leaked prospectus figures showed a ~$42bn 2025 net loss, most of it convertible-financing accounting.
  • FIS shipped its first Anthropic-built agent, an AML Investigations Agent now available to any US bank, with design partner Amalgamated projecting 90%-plus time savings on alert review.
  • Deals kept flowing to AI-native finance: InsurTech Ledgebrook raised $200m and regulated-finance OS Valon $150m, while the UK's stablecoin regime moved toward its authorisation gateway.

01

Key Takeaways
  1. Map your AI exposure on both sides of the balance sheet. The IMF's point is that banks finance the build-out and depend on it — your risk committee should see data-centre lending and internal AI reliance on one page.
  2. Treat "circular financing" as a named risk, not a headline. When AI firms are each other's customers and backers, a single stumble propagates — stress-test your exposure to the cluster, not just to one name.
  3. The payoff case is now concrete — cite it. Citi's 40%-faster, 30%-cheaper numbers are the benchmark your board will ask you to match; JPMorgan's "not yet visible in earnings" is the honest counterweight.
  4. Agents need identities before they get authority. The FIS agent ships with evidence citations and audit trails for a reason: an agent you can't reconstruct is one you can't defend to a supervisor.
  5. Read the Anthropic S-1 when it lands. A ~$42bn loss, mostly accounting, is exactly the kind of figure that will be quoted out of context — know the real number before a client does.
  6. The UK stablecoin window is opening now. With the authorisation gateway live, payments and treasury teams eyeing sterling tokens should engage before the 2027 regime hardens the rules.

02

Deals, Funding & M&A

Update: Anthropic's IPO Firms to November as Leaked Financials Show a $42bn Loss

The headline AI listing came into sharper focus. Anthropic reportedly held an investor day on 14 October, with Bloomberg pointing to marketing from the week of 9 November and trading before Thanksgiving — a public S-1, carrying the first audited numbers, expected around 23 October. Leaked prospectus reporting put 2025 revenue near $4.6bn against an $8bn operating loss and a ~$42bn net loss, roughly $34bn of it non-cash convertible-financing charges. Reported valuation talk spans $1.8–3tn; the New York Times says the raise could top $100bn.

So What: The $42bn figure will be weaponised by bulls and bears alike, so know what it is: mostly an accounting charge, not cash burned. The number that matters is the operating loss against revenue, and the price range in the public S-1. For FS investors, a $1.8tn-plus listing into the jittery tape below is the single event most likely to set — or break — AI-equity sentiment into year-end.

Ledgebrook Raises $200m to Scale AI-Native Specialty Underwriting

Boston InsurTech Ledgebrook raised $200m in primary equity, co-led by Allianz X and Rockefeller Capital Management, to expand its Blackbird underwriting engine for complex specialty risks. Specialty lines — the bespoke, judgement-heavy end of commercial insurance — have been the hardest for automation to reach, which is exactly why an AI-native underwriter drawing a strategic cheque from Allianz is notable. Alongside it, warranty-claims InsurTech Polygrade raised $6.3m, a reminder that AI is working down the full insurance stack, not just the headline lines.

So What: A major carrier's venture arm backing an AI-native specialty underwriter is a build-vs-buy signal for incumbents: Allianz is buying optionality on the segment it would struggle to rebuild in-house. Specialty and commercial underwriters should watch whether AI-native engines start winning on speed and loss ratio in the complex risks long considered too bespoke to automate — and plan for the day they do.

Valon Raises $150m at $2.3bn to Move Mortgage Servicing onto AI Agents

Valon Technologies raised a $150m Series D at a $2.3bn valuation, with Ribbit Capital joining, to migrate mortgage servicers onto ValonOS — an AI-native operating system for regulated finance, agents included. Mortgage servicing is a high-volume, rules-bound, compliance-heavy back office: the archetypal home for agentic automation, and a segment where legacy platforms are both costly and brittle. The raise extends a 2026 pattern of capital backing AI-native operating layers for the unglamorous, regulated middle of finance rather than flashy front-office tools.

So What: The money keeps favouring AI-native platforms that replace a regulated back office wholesale over point tools bolted onto legacy cores. For servicers and operations leaders, the competitive question is sharpening: can an incrementally-upgraded legacy stack match the cost curve of a rival running on an AI-native OS from the ground up? Where it can't, the migration case gets harder to defer each quarter.

03

New Products & Use Cases

FIS Ships an Anthropic-Built AML Investigations Agent to Any US Bank

FIS launched its AML Investigations Agent — the first product of its Anthropic partnership, built on Claude and available to any US bank, not just FIS customers. The agent pulls from core systems, payment records, check images and customer histories to assemble evidence for suspicious-activity investigations and draft recommendations that cite the underlying records, running in FIS's private cloud. Design partner Amalgamated Bank projects time savings above 90% on initial alert review, ~98% on deeper investigation and over 80% on SAR narratives — projections, not yet audited production results. Analysts still approve every case closure.

So What: Those time savings are eye-catching, but the design choices are the lesson: evidence citations, audit trails, human sign-off on closures. Accountability doesn't transfer to the vendor — Bank of America's $1.21bn in AML penalties is the reminder of what's at stake. Compliance leaders evaluating agentic AML should demand the same three controls as table stakes, and insist on live metrics: accuracy, and how often analysts overrule the agent.

The Agentic-Compliance Field Crowds — and Reaches UK Advice

FIS enters a fast-filling category: Fiserv's agentOS, NICE Actimize's agentic investigation tools and WorkFusion (now part of UiPath) for sanctions screening are all chasing the same regulated back-office work. The pattern repeats in wealth: UK adviser-workflow startup PlannerPal raised £4.3m to automate the compliance-heavy admin behind financial advice. Across both, the thesis is identical — AI's clearest near-term ROI in financial services sits in the document-heavy, rules-bound functions that are expensive to staff and painful to get wrong.

So What: Compliance and advice admin are becoming the most contested AI battlegrounds in FS because the ROI is measurable and the work is structured. For buyers, a crowding field is good news — more mature options, more pricing pressure — but it raises the switching-cost stakes. Pick for auditability and the ability to prove outcomes to a regulator, not for the longest feature list; the vendors will consolidate, and you want to be on a survivor.

04

Use Cases in Action

Q3 Bank Earnings: AI Shows Up in the Numbers — and on Both Sides of the Ledger

The mid-October bank results put concrete figures on AI for the first time. Citi said it now develops products up to 40% faster and has cut tech-vendor expenses 30% through coding tools, with CEO Jane Fraser calling the financial benefits well in excess of the year's investment — and warning "there's simply not enough compute." JPMorgan was more sober: CFO Jeremy Barnum said AI's benefit isn't yet visible in earnings at the bank's scale, while Jamie Dimon flagged work on tools to manage agentic-AI risk. On the financing side, Goldman's David Solomon conceded "no one knows" how compute demand will evolve.

So What: The split between Citi's hard numbers and JPMorgan's "not yet" is the real state of play — AI is delivering measurable productivity in pockets, but not yet at a scale that moves a megabank's P&L. Set your board's expectations there: fund the proven pockets, measure them honestly, and resist both the hype and the backlash. The firms that report specifics, as Citi did, will own the narrative.

Wall Street Becomes AI's Banker — and Inherits the Risk

The same earnings calls exposed the other half of the bet: banks are financing the AI build-out even as they adopt it. Morgan Stanley's Ted Pick called AI financing "existential" for the firm; CFO Sharon Yeshaya said it was "very discerning" on risk. Wells Fargo's Charlie Scharf flagged the heavy leverage behind data-centre projects, and Gabelli's Macrae Sykes warned credit exposure to speculative builds "could lead to issues down the road." It is the IMF's warning rendered in a loan book — concentrated, leveraged lending into an unproven demand curve.

So What: AI concentration risk isn't only an equity-book problem; it's increasingly a credit-book one, through data-centre and AI-infrastructure lending. Risk and treasury teams should map direct and indirect exposure to the AI capex cycle — loans, leveraged finance, counterparty links — and stress it against a demand shortfall, not just a rate move. The banks that can show that map to a supervisor will be glad they drew it early.

05

AI Equity Pulse: Bubble Watch

Weekly market analysis — written from the perspective of a senior equity analyst

Week in Numbers

Nasdaq Composite near a one-month low mid-week (−1.2% on 15 Oct) before an Oct 16 rebound  |  S&P 500 ~7,686  |  Dow ~51,900  |  10-yr Treasury ~5.09%, 30-yr ~5.43%  |  Hot September inflation — UBS now expects a third Fed hike  |  TSMC record Q3 profit on AI demand  |  IMF: AI correction a "salient risk"

The Watchdog Says It Plainly

This column has spent months circling the question the IMF has now put in print. In its October Global Financial Stability Report, the Fund named an AI-driven market correction a "salient risk" to global financial stability: valuations are stretched, investors are concentrated in a handful of names, and the sector runs on "circular financing" — AI firms acting as one another's investors, financiers and customers, so trouble at one can spread to all. The market obliged the thesis mid-week, with the Nasdaq sliding to a near one-month low on AI and rate fears, before a bond-led rebound on 16 October. The IMF is careful to say markets remain resilient and its bank stress tests hold up. But the frame has shifted from "is this a bubble?" to "who is exposed if it pops?"

Rates Rejoin the Story

The macro backdrop is turning against the richest multiples again. September inflation ran hot, the 10-year yield sits above 5% and the 30-year above 5.4%, and UBS now expects a third Fed hike this year. For long-duration AI names, that is a direct valuation headwind layered on top of the IMF's structural warning. Yet the demand data refuses to break: TSMC posted record Q3 profit on AI orders, and Citi says there is "not enough compute." That is the standoff — fundamentals that keep validating the spend, against a cost of capital and a concentration profile that keep raising the stakes.

Analyst View

When the IMF puts "AI correction" and "financial stability" in the same sentence, it changes the conversation for FS firms specifically — because a supervisor who reads that report will ask you about your exposure. The useful work now is not predicting the top; it is mapping transmission. Circular financing means the risk is in the cluster, not the single stock, and the bank earnings this week showed it reaching the credit book as well as the equity book. Two dated catalysts sit just ahead: hyperscaler Q3 earnings in late October, the real test of whether capex is still accelerating, and the Anthropic listing in November, the first public price on a pure frontier lab. Either could set the tone into year-end. Position for a rate shock and a sentiment shock at once, keep AI-linked exposure sized to survive both, and — above all — be able to show your regulator the map before they ask for it.

06

Regulation & Compliance

The IMF Hands Regulators a Watch-List — Concentration, Leverage, Cloud, Cyber

Beyond the market warning, the IMF's GFSR set an agenda for supervisors: watch excessive borrowing, concentrated investment, and cyber resilience. It flagged banks' heavy reliance on the same few cloud and software providers as a shared point of failure that an attack could turn into disruption of payments, trading and deposit access — and noted that more than 70% of vulnerabilities exploited in 2026 were being abused at or before public disclosure, a window more capable AI models are shortening. It is the clearest signal yet that AI and third-party concentration are becoming core prudential concerns, not technology-team ones.

So What: The GFSR is a preview of the questions coming in your next supervisory dialogue. Third-party and cloud concentration, AI-accelerated cyber exposure, and AI-linked credit risk are moving onto the prudential agenda together. Operational-resilience and model-risk teams should get ahead of them now — a concentration map of your cloud and AI dependencies, and a cyber posture built for machine-speed exploitation, are fast becoming expected, not exemplary.

UK Stablecoins Move From Sandbox to Authorisation Gateway

The UK's stablecoin regime advanced another step. Following the FCA's sandbox cohort — which included Revolut among four firms testing sterling-token issuance, payments and wholesale settlement — the authorisation gateway has opened, with final rules expected to firm up before the full crypto regime goes live in 2027. The sandbox work, run alongside the Bank of England's Digital Securities Sandbox, is explicitly designed to let the FCA test its proposed policy in a live market before locking it in. For a jurisdiction courting agentic-payments and tokenised-settlement activity, it is a deliberate move to build the rails inside the regulatory perimeter.

So What: The UK is sequencing deliberately — sandbox, then authorisation, then regime — which gives firms a rare chance to shape rules before they bind. Payments and treasury teams weighing sterling stablecoins for settlement should engage with the gateway now rather than waiting for 2027; the agentic-payment rails this newsletter has tracked all year will run on exactly this framework, and early movers help write it.

07

New Partnerships

FIS × Anthropic: A Core-Systems Giant Puts a Frontier Lab Inside the Bank Stack

The AML agent is the first visible output of a deeper alliance: FIS — one of the banking industry's largest core and payments providers — building on Anthropic's Claude and distributing the result to banks well beyond its own customer base, with Amalgamated Bank as design partner. The structure matters as much as the product. Rather than each bank integrating a frontier model itself, a trusted core vendor wraps the model in private-cloud hosting, governance and audit, and ships it as a bank-ready product. It is the systems-integrator route to frontier AI, at core-provider scale.

So What: For most banks, frontier AI will arrive through incumbents they already trust — core providers, not direct model contracts — because the governance, hosting and distribution come pre-solved. That makes your core and payments vendors strategic AI partners whether you planned it or not. Technology leaders should ask their core providers for their frontier-AI roadmap now, and weigh it as a real factor in renewal decisions.

08

Research & Talent Moves

The Payoff Clock Starts Ticking — "By the End of Next Year"

This week sharpened the question the whole sector is circling: when does AI spend show up in results? JPMorgan's Jeremy Barnum conceded the benefit isn't yet visible in earnings at the bank's scale, while Zacks' Brian Mulberry framed the deadline bluntly — investors will question the AI story if productivity gains and lower costs haven't arrived "by the end of next year." Citi's hard numbers show the gains are real where they land; the open question is whether they scale from pockets to the P&L before patience runs out.

So What: There is now an informal market deadline — end of 2027 — for AI to prove itself in FS financials. For anyone sponsoring an AI programme, that is the window to convert pilots into measurable, reported outcomes. Instrument the ROI from the start, report it in the language of cost-to-serve and cycle time, and bank the pockets that work; "transformational someday" is wearing thin as a business case.

Bank AI Adoption Gets Measured — the Trackers Mature

As the payoff debate sharpens, the tools to score it are maturing. Benchmarks such as the Evident AI Index — which ranks the world's largest banks on AI maturity across talent, innovation, leadership and transparency — are becoming the external yardstick executives and investors use to judge who is actually ahead, rather than who talks the most. In a week when Citi quantified its gains and JPMorgan admitted its haven't landed, independent adoption scoring is exactly the lens the market is reaching for.

So What: External AI-maturity rankings are becoming reputational infrastructure — cited by investors, analysts and recruits. If your firm is benchmarked by one, treat its methodology as a roadmap: talent depth, shipped use cases, leadership signals and transparency are what move the score. Knowing how you rank, and why, is becoming part of the AI strategy itself, not a vanity metric.
