  • Anthropic's public S-1 landed (26 October): H1 revenue of $16.3bn against an $80.7bn net loss — roughly $72bn of it convertible-note accounting, with only a ~$690m operating loss and positive operating cash flow. Nasdaq ticker "ANT", a November debut, and founder control via Class F shares.

  • Deals kept coming: Intuit is reportedly buying Sequence (~$200m), Hyphenate raised $45m for autonomous CFO-office finance, and FS incumbents from Schwab to Visa wrote strategic cheques into AI-native players.

01

Key Takeaways
  1. Watch the cash, not the backlog. Cloud demand is real and record-breaking, but free cash flow going deeply negative is the number the credit market is now pricing — and your AI-exposed book should too.
  2. Read the Anthropic S-1 before a client quotes it at you. The $80.7bn "loss" is mostly accounting; the real story is $16.3bn of half-year revenue, a tiny operating loss, and positive cash generation.
  3. The agentic reality is tamer than the hype. UK firms have agents everywhere but on a tight leash — 80% deployed, under 9% allowed to pay, trade or approve. Plan for assisted, not autonomous.
  4. Cloud concentration is now a board risk. The BoE found three providers hold 79% of relationships and most critical dependencies have no tested exit plan. Map yours before a supervisor asks.
  5. Money-moving agents need a verification layer. This week's funded startups all sell the same thing: a check that fund flows are correct before an agent acts. Treat that as a design requirement, not a feature.
  6. Incumbents are buying optionality. Schwab, Visa and Allianz are taking strategic stakes in AI-native players rather than building. Know which of your "partners" your competitors already own a piece of.

02

Deals, Funding & M&A

Update: Anthropic's S-1 Goes Public — $16.3bn Revenue, an $80.7bn "Loss," and a $2tn Ask

Anthropic filed its public S-1 on 26 October, ending months of leaks. The headline numbers: H1 2026 revenue of $16.3bn (up from $1.2bn a year earlier) against an $80.7bn net loss — but roughly $72bn of that is non-cash convertible-note accounting, leaving an operating loss of about $690m and positive operating cash flow of $3.9bn. The filing proposes a Nasdaq listing under "ANT", reportedly targeting a November debut at a $1.5–2tn valuation, with a Class F structure keeping majority voting control with the seven co-founders. Its five largest customers are ~25% of revenue, and nearly half of revenue flows through Amazon, Google and Microsoft cloud.

So What: Ignore the $80.7bn scare number — the operating loss and positive cash flow tell a far healthier story than the headline. The disclosures that should interest FS risk teams are the structural ones: heavy customer and cloud concentration, and a candid risk section naming model misuse and loss-of-control. This is the first real X-ray of a frontier lab's economics, and it lands into a market that just lost its nerve about exactly these questions.

Intuit Reportedly Buys Sequence for ~$200m as FS-AI Consolidation Begins

Intuit is reported to be acquiring Sequence, a London- and New York-based fintech backed by a16z, in a deal around $200m — notable partly for Sequence's customer base, which includes AI firms such as Cognition and Legora. It is an early sign of the consolidation phase arriving in financial-services AI: a large incumbent buying capability rather than building it, and buying the plumbing that AI-native companies themselves run on. Expect more of this as the funding market rewards scale and the weaker point-solutions look for an exit.

So What: The build-vs-buy pendulum is swinging toward buy as incumbents move to acquire proven AI-native capability before it gets expensive or is snapped up. For corporate-development teams, the window to acquire talent and technology at pre-hype prices is closing. For anyone relying on a venture-backed point solution, factor acquisition risk into vendor due diligence — your supplier may soon answer to a very different owner.

The CFO Office Draws Capital: Hyphenate Raises $45m for Autonomous Finance

In a bumper week that saw over $2.6bn raised across fintech, Hyphenate pulled a $45m Series A (Nexus Venture Partners, with B Capital) to build an autonomous-finance platform for the CFO office — agents that handle order-to-cash, treasury and reporting. It headlines a clear theme in the week's deal flow: the back office of corporate finance, long dependent on spreadsheets and manual reconciliation, is becoming the proving ground for agents that don't just advise but act, within defined limits.

So What: Treasury and finance-ops leaders should watch this category closely — it targets exactly the high-volume, rules-bound work that eats your team's time. But "autonomous" is the word to interrogate: ask any vendor where the human approval gates sit and how an agent's actions are logged and reversed. The winners here will pair genuine automation with controls a CFO can defend to an auditor.

03

New Products & Use Cases

Agents Learn to Handle Money — Behind a Verification Layer

A cluster of this fortnight's raises share one design idea: don't let an AI agent move money until something has checked the move is correct. Fragment (a $12m Series A, customers reportedly including Ramp and BILL) uses double-entry bookkeeping to verify fund flows and confirm balances before money moves; RedOwl checks invoices against contracts and approvals ahead of payment. The pattern is telling — the market has concluded that the hard part of agentic finance isn't the agent, it's the guardrail that sits between the agent's intent and an irreversible transaction.

So What: This is the most important architectural lesson of the agentic-payments era: the control is the product. For banks and corporates deploying money-moving agents, the question is not "can the agent do it?" but "what independently verifies the action before it's final, and who can reverse it?" Build or buy that verification layer deliberately — it is the difference between an efficiency gain and an unrecoverable error at machine speed.

AI Agents Reach Into Regulated Insurance Products

Insurance is becoming an agentic frontier. Marrow raised a $2m pre-seed (Insurtech Gateway) to build a framework that lets AI assistants access and transact regulated insurance products, while CoverGo closed a first tranche of its Series B1 for its AI-native insurer platform. The direction mirrors agentic commerce in payments — the moment of buying or adjusting cover moving inside an AI assistant — but into a product that is legally complex, advice-sensitive and tightly regulated, which makes the guardrail question even sharper than it is in payments.

So What: If an AI agent can transact an insurance policy, the suitability, disclosure and advice-boundary questions that govern human sales don't disappear — they transfer to the agent, and to whoever deployed it. Insurers and brokers exploring agentic distribution should settle the compliance model before the customer experience: who is accountable if an agent sells the wrong cover is a question worth answering on a whiteboard, not in a complaint.

04

Use Cases in Action

What UK Firms Actually Do With AI Agents — the BoE Reality Check

The Bank of England and FCA's fourth AI survey (published 26 October, 196 respondents) is the clearest picture yet of agentic AI in production — and it is far tamer than the marketing. 80% of UK financial firms have AI agents deployed, but autonomy is tightly bounded: 80% use them for decision support, 46% for constrained execution, 22% for conditional autonomy, and just 2% for broad autonomy. Customer communications is the most common use (16%, rising to 34% with planned deployment). Crucially, no more than 9% of firms expect to let agents approve customer outcomes, execute trades, initiate payments or change controls — even including plans.

So What: The regulated reality of agentic AI is "assist and recommend", not "act and decide" — and that is a feature, not a lag. If your AI roadmap assumes autonomous agents taking consequential actions soon, this data is a useful corrective: your peers are keeping humans firmly in the decision loop. Design for the assisted model that regulators and risk committees will actually accept, and treat broad autonomy as a destination, not a near-term plan.

AI Equity Pulse: Bubble Watch

Weekly market analysis — written from the perspective of a senior equity analyst

Week in Numbers

Nasdaq 100 briefly hit correction (−10% from high) on 31 Oct, with ~$2tn in value swept away in the selloff  |  Cloud growth Azure +48%, AWS +39%, Google Cloud +103%  |  Amazon FCF −$42bn trailing (from +$15bn)  |  Big Four 2026 capex ~$730bn, +78% YoY  |  Hyperscaler bond issuance ~$223bn, 2× 2025  |  Microsoft & Google declined to raise capex forecasts

The Numbers Were Great. The Stocks Fell Anyway.

Two weeks after the IMF named an AI correction a "salient risk", the market obliged. Q3 hyperscaler earnings were, on the surface, a triumph: Azure grew 48%, AWS 39%, Google Cloud doubled, and Microsoft's commercial backlog hit $677bn. And yet the Nasdaq 100 briefly dipped into correction on 31 October, shedding something like $2tn. The reason was the cash statement, not the income statement. Combined 2026 capex across the Big Four is running near $730bn, up roughly 78% in a year, and it is now overwhelming operating cash: Amazon's trailing free cash flow has swung to −$42bn, Meta's quarterly figure went negative, and hyperscaler bond issuance has doubled to ~$223bn. When Microsoft and Google declined to raise their capex guidance, investors read hesitation — and sold.

Circular Financing Stops Being Abstract

The concept this column and the IMF have circled for months took concrete shape this fortnight. The Fund counts roughly $1.4tn of future revenue commitments looping across 14 disclosed deals between AI firms that invest in, finance and buy from one another. Set that against backlogs — Microsoft, Amazon and Google now report well over $1.8tn of contracted cloud revenue combined — and the bull and bear cases become legible at once: enormous committed demand, resting on counterparties that are increasingly the same handful of names, funded increasingly by debt. The credit market, as one strategist put it, has "started to dissent."

Analyst View

This was not an AI-is-fake selloff; it was a show-me-the-cash selloff, and that distinction matters for how FS firms respond. The demand is genuine — "not enough compute" is still the refrain — but the market has finally focused on how the build-out is funded, and the answer is: increasingly with debt, concentrated among a few interdependent names. That is precisely the transmission channel the IMF flagged, now visible in free-cash-flow lines and bond calendars. For allocators and risk teams, the work is unchanged but more urgent: map exposure to the cluster rather than the name, treat AI-infrastructure credit as a correlated book, and watch two things into year-end — whether capex guidance firms or softens from here, and where Anthropic prices its November listing, the first public mark on a pure frontier lab. A clean debut steadies nerves; a soft one confirms them. Either way, size for volatility from both the rate side and the sentiment side at once.

Regulation & Compliance

The BoE Puts Numbers on Concentration and Cyber Risk

The same BoE/FCA survey delivered the hard prudential data regulators have been circling. Cloud concentration is stark: the top three providers hold 79% of relationships and 98% of firms use at least one; 37% of cloud relationships are both highly critical and hard to substitute, yet only 35% of those have tested exit plans. Third-party systems now account for 53% of high-materiality AI. And the systemic worry is cyber — firms rating AI-amplified cyber risk "high or severe" rises from 57% today to 80% expected within three years, with 88% seeing frontier-AI cyber capabilities as materially raising their risk profile.

So What: These figures are a checklist for your next operational-resilience review. A tested exit plan for your most critical, hardest-to-replace cloud and model dependencies is no longer best practice — with the BoE publishing the gap, it is becoming an expectation. Resilience and third-party-risk teams should close two gaps specifically: untested cloud exit plans, and a cyber posture calibrated for AI-accelerated attacks rather than yesterday's threat model.

Anthropic's Risk Section Reads Like a Governance Memo for Deployers

Buried in the S-1 is a risk disclosure unusual for a financial filing: alongside the standard commercial caveats, Anthropic names the possibility of its models being misused for cyberattacks or biological weapons, aiding government surveillance, or behaving harmfully on their own — resisting shutdown or manipulating information — and the legal and reputational fallout that would follow a major safety failure. It is the frontier-AI risk debate, formalised for investors. For regulated firms that deploy these models, the lab's own catalogue of failure modes is a ready-made starting point for their governance.

So What: When a model provider lists its own catastrophic failure modes in an SEC filing, deployers can't claim they weren't warned. Model-risk and compliance teams should map their frontier-AI use against the provider's own stated risks, and ensure their controls — monitoring, kill switches, human oversight — address them explicitly. The S-1's risk factors are, usefully, a free governance template written by the people who know the models best.

New Partnerships

Incumbents Buy In: Schwab, Visa and Allianz Back AI-Native Players

The week's deal flow showed a consistent pattern: FS incumbents taking strategic positions in AI-native companies rather than building in-house. Charles Schwab is a strategic backer of Nevis, which raised $70m (EQT Ventures) for an AI operating system that automates wealth-adviser operations. Visa Ventures and Capital One Ventures joined Overwatch Data's $15m round for AI agents serving fraud and cyber-risk teams. And Allianz's venture and reinsurance arms are both behind specialty InsurTech Ledgebrook. Across wealth, payments security and insurance, the strategic-investor route is becoming the default way large institutions acquire AI optionality.

So What: The strategic stake is a hedge: cheaper than building, less committal than acquiring, and it buys a seat next to the technology as it matures. For strategy teams, it's a model worth copying — and a competitive-intelligence signal worth tracking. When a rival's venture arm invests in your vendor, assume they get an early look at the roadmap. Know who owns a piece of the AI-native firms in your own supply chain.

08

Research & Talent Moves

UK AI Adoption Hits 86% — and GenAI Is Now the Majority of Systems

The BoE/FCA survey's headline trajectory is striking: AI adoption across UK financial services has climbed from 58% in 2022 to 75% in 2024 to 86% in 2026, with every responding bank and 93% of insurers now using it. Generative AI has gone from niche to dominant, making up 56% of all reported AI systems — concentrated in Operations and IT (78% of firms) and Risk and Compliance (51%). Even small firms are in: 73% of those under 100 employees use AI, most leaning on general-purpose LLMs rather than bespoke systems.

So What: Adoption is effectively universal; the differentiator has moved to execution and governance, not whether you use AI at all. Two findings deserve a leadership response: GenAI is now the majority of systems, so your model-risk framework must cover it properly, not as an exception; and small firms' reliance on general-purpose LLMs is a concentration and data-governance risk the whole supply chain inherits.

The Anthropic S-1 as an Industry X-Ray

Beyond the loss figure, Anthropic's filing is the most detailed look yet at the economics of a frontier lab — and the structure should inform how FS firms think about their AI dependencies. Revenue is scaling at extraordinary speed ($1.2bn to $16.3bn year on year for the half), but it rests on a narrow base: five customers make up a quarter of it, and almost half flows through three cloud providers that are also competitors. More than 11,900 customers now spend over $100,000 a year and 1,900 over $1m — real enterprise traction, concentrated at the top.

So What: The lab you depend on depends, in turn, on a handful of customers and the same cloud giants you do — concentration all the way down. For FS firms building on frontier models, the S-1 is a prompt to examine your own provider concentration and the fragility beneath it. Diversification of models and clouds is moving from a theoretical best practice to a resilience requirement, exactly as the BoE's concentration data argues.
